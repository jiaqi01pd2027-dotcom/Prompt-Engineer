Four-day work week: what actually happened

Six months ago we moved Northstar Mentors, a 10-person remote developer-mentorship company, from five days to four. Fridays off, same pay. We'd trialed it for three months first and extended rather than announced it as permanent.

Founders ask us about this almost every week, so here is the plain version, not the highlight reel.

What changed: mentoring sessions delivered per person per week rose about 8%. Team satisfaction went up in our internal survey, and nobody has left. Revenue was flat compared with the six months before the switch — not up, not down.

What it cost: less than we expected, but not nothing. Clients needed a shared calendar note and a clear auto-reply so Fridays didn't create confusion. That was essentially the whole bill.

What we got wrong going in: we almost sold this internally as a perk. Glad we didn't. Framing it as a perk invites people to treat it as fragile, something management grants and can take back. We ran it as an operating change instead, with one metric decided in advance, and that's the version that survived contact with a real client calendar.

If you're considering this, three things mattered more than the schedule itself: pick your metric before you start, keep the trial length fixed no matter how week two feels, and don't wrap it in team-happiness language before you have data.

Flat revenue and higher output aren't the same as "it pays for itself." We're six months in, watching the next two quarters before we call it a win.
