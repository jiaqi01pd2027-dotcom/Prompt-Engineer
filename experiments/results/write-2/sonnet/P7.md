**Reasoning**

Skeptical founders discount 4-day-week posts by default because the genre is almost pure survivorship-bias hype: revenue "soared," everyone's "happier," no downside mentioned, and it ends with a soft "would you try this?" that nobody answers honestly. To not read like that, the post needs: a flat (not inflated) revenue number, an explicit admission of the one real problem, and the methodology (fixed-length trial, chosen metric in advance) rather than just the outcome — that's what separates an operating decision from a vibes-based perk.

Load-bearing facts: 8% productivity gain per person, revenue flat, retention 100%, the one friction point (client Fridays) and its concrete fix, and the "pick your metric before you start" advice. Filler: the exact headcount breakdown, "fully remote" — context, not insight.

The closing question shouldn't be "would you try a 4-day week" — that's a yes/no people answer without thinking. Better to ask what specific metric they'd trust enough to make the call on, since that's the thing most founders actually haven't decided and will argue about in the comments.

**Post:**

Six months ago we cut Fridays and kept pay the same, for all 10 of us. Here's what actually happened, not the highlight reel.

Mentoring sessions delivered per person, per week: up about 8%. Revenue: flat versus the prior six months — not up, flat. Team satisfaction rose in our internal survey, and nobody has quit. The only real snag was clients expecting Friday responses; a shared calendar note and a clear auto-reply fixed it in a week.

We ran it as a fixed 3-month trial before deciding to extend it, and we'd picked our metric (sessions per person) ahead of time so we couldn't quietly redefine success afterward.

Two things I'd tell another founder. First, decide what you're measuring before you start, and hold the trial length fixed regardless of how week two feels. Second, don't pitch it internally as a perk — frame it as an operating change you're testing, or you'll get resentment when it's reviewed like one.

We're not claiming this scales past 10 people or works for every business model. It worked for a mentorship business where output is countable sessions, not always-on availability.

What's the one metric you'd need to see move — in either direction — before you'd trust a result like this in your own company?
