**Reasoning**

- What sceptical founders want: not "everyone is happier" but whether output held, whether revenue held, what broke, and whether they would do it again. Revenue is the first thing they will look for, so hiding it or burying it kills credibility.
- Facts that carry the post: sessions per person up ~8%, revenue flat, nobody left, the Friday client friction and its cheap fix, and the three-point advice (metric first, fixed-length trial, operating change not perk). The team composition adds credibility in one line. "Satisfaction up in an internal survey" is soft and should be mentioned once, not led with.
- What would make it generic: leading with happiness, calling it a game changer, quietly omitting flat revenue, no numbers, and ending with "would you try it?" which gets polite nods rather than replies.
- The question: ask the people who said no. "What number stopped you?" invites a specific, honest answer from exactly the sceptics the post is for.

**Post:**

Six months ago Northstar Mentors moved to a four-day week. Ten people, fully remote, Fridays off, same pay. Here is what the numbers say.

Mentoring sessions delivered per person per week are up about 8%. Revenue is flat against the six months before the switch. Nobody has left, and satisfaction is up in our internal survey. The first two are the ones I would ask about if I were you.

So: more output per person, same money. We did not get a growth story. We got the same business run in four days instead of five, with a team that does not want to go back.

What went wrong: nothing we did not expect. The only real friction was clients trying to reach us on Fridays. A shared calendar note and a clear auto-reply fixed it.

Three things I would tell another founder:

1. Pick the metric you will watch before you start. Ours was sessions per person. If you decide afterwards, you will pick whichever number looks best.

2. Keep the trial fixed-length. We did three months, then extended it on purpose. An open-ended trial is just a policy you have not admitted to.

3. Do not sell it internally as a perk. Perks get cut when things get tight. We treated it as an operating change, which meant everyone owned making it work.

Ten people is small, and mentoring is a business where output is easy to count. Your case may be harder.

If you looked at a four-day week and decided against it, what was the number that stopped you?
