**What sceptical founders want to know:** Did output actually hold, or did you just move the work? What did it cost in revenue? What broke? And how would I run the test myself without fooling myself.

**Facts that carry the post:** revenue flat (the honest one that buys credibility for everything else), 8% sessions per person, nobody left, and the three pieces of advice — those are the transferable part. The 3-month trial that was extended is worth naming because it shows a decision procedure, not a leap.

**Filler:** the 6/2/2 headcount split, "internal survey" as a phrase. The Friday auto-reply detail sounds like filler but earns its place — it's the concrete, small answer to "what broke," and small is the point.

**What would make it generic:** burnout-and-recharge language, "the future of work," five numbered lessons, a claim that output went *up* without mentioning revenue, and any implication this generalises past a 10-person remote team.

**The question:** not "would you try this?" — that gets performative yeses. Ask what would make them stop. A pre-commitment question forces a specific number and gets real replies.

## Post:

Six months ago we moved Northstar Mentors to a four-day week. Fridays off, same pay, ten people, fully remote. Here is what the numbers say.

Mentoring sessions delivered per person per week are up about 8%. Revenue is flat against the six months before the switch. Internal satisfaction is up, and nobody has left.

The flat revenue is the number I'd want if I were reading this. We did not unlock growth. We bought back a day a week and held output steady. That's a good result, not a miracle, and any post claiming otherwise is selling something.

The problems were the ones we expected. Clients sometimes needed us on a Friday. A shared calendar note and a clear auto-reply covered it. That was the entire operational cost.

Three things I'd tell another founder:

Pick your metric before you start. We chose sessions per mentor per week. If you decide afterwards what counted as success, you will always find a number that says yes.

Keep the trial fixed-length. Ours was three months, then we extended it deliberately. An open-ended trial quietly becomes a policy nobody agreed to.

Don't sell it internally as a perk. We framed it as an operating change with conditions attached. Perks are almost impossible to withdraw. Operating changes can be revised without anyone feeling robbed.

Six months in, I'd do it again. The honest caveat is that ten people is an easy size to try this at.
