Six months ago we moved Northstar Mentors, a fully remote developer-mentorship company of ten people, from a five-day week to a four-day week. Fridays off, same pay. We ran it as a three-month trial first, looked at the numbers, and extended it.

Output went up. Mentoring sessions delivered per person per week rose about 8 percent, which is the one hard number we have. Team satisfaction rose in our internal survey, and nobody has left since the change.

Revenue was flat compared with the six months before the switch, so this was not a growth lever for us. The problems were smaller than we expected. Coordinating with clients on Fridays needed a shared calendar note and a clear auto-reply, and that was the whole fix. Nothing broke that we had not already planned for.

Three things we would tell another founder. Decide the metric you will watch before you start, because you will be tempted to define success after the fact. Keep the trial to a fixed length with a real decision at the end, not an open-ended experiment that quietly becomes permanent. And do not sell it internally as a perk. We framed it as an operating change, which meant the team felt free to say what was not working instead of defending a benefit they were afraid of losing.

Six months in, our honest read is that a four-day week made the work denser and the team steadier, and left the top line where it was. That may or may not be a good trade for your business.

If you have considered a four-day week and decided against it, what stopped you?
